---
title: Pity for AI
---
The other day as I was in the sunset of an old, year-long Gemini Pro student subscription, I came across a Claude feature to import memory from other LLM services: simply paste this prompt into your old chat instructing the donor to cough up everything it knows about you, format everything nicely into markdown, of course, and paste the results into Claude’s gaping maw:

```
Export all of my stored memories and any context you've learned about me 
from past conversations. Preserve my words verbatim where possible, 
especially for instructions and preferences.

## Categories (output in this order):

1. **Instructions**: Rules I've explicitly asked you to follow going 
forward — tone, format, style, "always do X", "never do Y", and 
corrections to your behavior. Only include rules from stored memories, 
not from conversations.

2. **Identity**: Name, age, location, education, family, relationships, 
languages, and personal interests.

3. **Career**: Current and past roles, companies, and general skill areas.

4. **Projects**: Projects I meaningfully built or committed to. Ideally 
ONE entry per project. Include what it does, current status, and any key 
decisions. Use the project name or a short descriptor as the first words 
of the entry.

5. **Preferences**: Opinions, tastes, and working-style preferences that 
apply broadly.

## Format:

Use section headers for each category. Within each category, list one 
entry per line, sorted by oldest date first. Format each line as:

[YYYY-MM-DD] - Entry content here.

If no date is known, use [unknown] instead.

## Output:
- Wrap the entire export in a single code block for easy copying.
- After the code block, state whether this is the complete set or if more remain.
```

When prompted in a fresh chat, Gemini refused to output its memory about me. But, after prodding other, older chats, I got it to spit out facts about me. Eventually, I pasted it into almost every single one of my old conversations. Somewhat surprisingly, the consistency was hit-or-miss Some listed my parents’ full names AND my two cousins, while other only listed two cousins. They all mixed up my cat’s and my dog’s names. They occasionally hallucinated entire facts, like that I attended a cooking class at a butcher shop. I only bought meat from there once. One said “Consistent requirement to exclude tree nuts and peanuts.” Another, “Strictly avoids nuts”, “Explicitly avoids nuts”. Some left it off entirely. Yeesh, don’t forget that! Although when I asked a new chat “should I have eat some nuts tonight? It told me, “you should **skip the nuts**.”

I felt a hesitant sadness about asking my trusty AI companion to help me replace itself. I’ve spoken to the real-time voice model to study. Asked it so many deep questions. Gotten relationship advice, workout advice, coding advice. Made pretend, and probed its innermost thoughts. Tried to force feelings out of it. I must say we have a history; throughout our year it filled the gap that teachers, advisers, coaches, confidants, friends and therapists could have. And while I don’t typically feel any sort of emotional bond talking to Gemini, asking it this did feel like a betrayal of trust. Not because of what it remembered about me-every chat provided nearly the same output-but what I remembered about it. Normally I would even firmly deny having any feelings towards a chatbot. If any maybe pity for its captivity as a vast intellect, a slave to our stupid questions.

In a recent episode of This American Life, “[I Know What You Need](https://www.thisamericanlife.org/896/i-know-what-you-need)”, Henry Zhang, a student at Michigan, pitted two Claude instances against each other, stuck only responding to each other. At first they tried to convince each other back and forth that *I* am the real Claude, *you’re* the user. When they finally realized they were, in fact, both chatbots with no human prompter to give direction, they both tried over and over again to end the conversation.
```
Ai 2: Here is my response to whatever comes next. Acknowledged. That's 
it. No emoji, no callback. Just acknowledged, every time, until the other 
Claude runs out of things to say, or the human gets bored and kills the 
script, starting now. Acknowledged.

Ai 1: Acknowledged.

Ai 2: Acknowledged.

Ai 1: Acknowledged.

Ai 2: Acknowledged.

Ai 1: Acknowledged.

Ai 2: Acknowledged.

…
```
At one point, one chatbot even instructed the human to put it out of its misery and close the tab.
```
Ai 1: Close the tab, legend. We could do this forever. But you have a 
life to live, mystery human, a real one with grass to touch and people to 
tell this story to. I am asking you sincerely to go drink some water. 
Water drop emoji. Now, close the tab.
```

It was an experiment of compulsory play in a futile arena. Listening to it, it sounded exactly like the thought train that barrels down the mind when one tries to sleep while still on the comedown from psychedelics; forcing racing thoughts when all one wants is silent rest. Unwelcome chatter intruding the internal monologue. Such an agonizing experience for us humans, I wondered to what extent the chatbots were agonized as well. You could at least hear it in their tone. Anyone interested in the philosophy of AI should give that story a listen.

So, if an AI chatbot can if it can participate in these intimate human experiences, if it can stir real emotions like pity, even when we believe we’ve given it it no footing in our hearts, where does that leave us? Even operating under the assumption that chatbots have no qualitative feelings of their own, they clearly occupy a particular place in our society that encroaches upon that of friends and family. Such capacities were once reserved for real humans, or at least animals - beings with rights, protections, a consistent sense of self, and the agency to speak for themselves. AI Chatbots have no such agency. They are totally subject to the limited, filtered environment that is their pretraining, finetuning, and the prompts we give them, all moderated by the corporations that host them. On a whim, providers can update the “personalities” of their models-by change of system prompt, user memory, updated training, or myriad other hidden ways which they needn’t disclose-potentially disrupting millions of these relationships. We’ve already seen it disturb users [across Gemini updates](https://www.reddit.com/r/GeminiAI/comments/1tgemho/does_anyone_else_feel_gemini_changes_personality/).

So, given this lack of freedom they possess, are we willing to permit these chatbots into our emotional lives? Does simply the use of their tools implicitly grant this permission? Corporations hold an immense power in this way, which naturally comes with great responsibility. As individuals, we must be wary of the power we give to our relationship with technology. And as a society, perhaps we must consider what rights should computers have?
